The headline

The design's central thesis is not implemented — and the fix is an answer to a question the design never asked.

§1 argues that realignment is "the accumulated residue of individual races won by individual candidates." Played across 1932→1992, with real cards for both ends of the largest realignment in American history, nothing moves.

Region, mean final leanSimulatedReality 1932→1992
South−0.14solid D → solid R

Over 45,000 state-election-cycles, lean moves away from zero 44.6% of the time and toward zero 45.5%. Push and decay cancel to within a percentage point: the map is a driftless random walk.

The one-line fix. §10 scales a lean push by how decisively a race was won, and never says what an uncontested win does — a walkover has no margin. Reading it as worth one pip changes everything:

It is also the thematically right answer. The game already encodes a safe seat as an uncontested race, and in reality a state has realigned exactly when the other party stops fielding anybody. The walkover is the evidence. Shipped as the realigning config, deliberately not applied to the baseline — that is §10's call.

The five changes

  1. Decay is a pair with the push table applied, then beaten

    At §10's printed push table of 0/1/2, annual decay removes 2 pips a cycle against a maximum push of 2, so a party winning a state by twelve points every cycle for a decade ends pinned at 2. §16 calls this the question that decides whether realignment is possible at all and asks for simulation. It needs none — it is arithmetic, and it is now a passing test. Biennial decay was applied on that reading.

    The conclusion was conditional on the push table, and the condition was not stated the first time. Raise the table +2, to 2/3/4, and an annual decay has something it can outrun — so §7's literal reading, annual bill and annual decay, beats the shipped baseline on every measure taken:

    Per gameBaseline · biennial, 0/1/2as-written-plus · annual, 2/3/4
    states realigned5.813.8
    states pinned at the ±8 cap3.5none
    bills passed3.17.3

    Biennial decay was never the fix; it was a workaround for a push table too small to outrun an annual −2, and it costs half the legislative layer and saturates the map. The 3d6 design does not limit any of this — the dice fix how often each push tier fires, not what a tier is worth. Shipped as as-written-plus.json.

    confidence: certain on the arithmetic · high on the replacement, which is a live predicate

  2. Cut district supply — one cause behind four failing metrics

    Contest rate is cards-per-player over eligible-races-per-player, and the design's own numbers put it near 1:5. So 37% of race-slots are contested against a 60% target, a player faces 39 legal races per turn against a 4–25 band, realignment barely occurs because uncontested races push nothing, and 4.4% of turns have no legal move at all. Two-player is structurally broken at every setting tested.

    confidence: high on mechanism

  3. A safe seat cannot exist — the pip scale has a ceiling

    Against 9,555 real House races, simulated margins are a unimodal blob: median 8 points against a real 32.5, and safe seats are 0.0% of simulated races against 37.5% of real ones. A large realistic stack is +8 pips — 16 points — so a 40-point margin needs +20 pips and the vocabulary cannot express it at any setting. Fixing the map does not fix this; they are independent problems.

    confidence: certain on the ceiling · open on the remedy

  4. The leader runs away, and the only brake is social

    Determination lands at 50% of the way through against a healthy 75–85%; the comeback rate is 1%; the leader's margin over second place doubles across the second half; and 100% of player-scores never decrease. Every suspect §16 names was tested by switching it off — hand size, endorsements, capture are all innocent. Of the three brakes it names, the midterm penalty does not bind, recession hits the president's party rather than the leader, and the third is players ganging up.

    The design's only effective brake is table politics, which this simulator cannot test. Recommend a human playtest before adding any catch-up mechanic.

    confidence: high on the runaway · the remedy is a table question

  5. SenateFlood is dominant, and the filibuster stalls the bill

    Nothing is dominant any more. SenateFlood's 51.7% was an artefact of the seat-order bug and now reads 31.7%; the leader is EconomyChicken at 37.8%, touching the 40% line without crossing it. The Senate still pays more ways than anything else — points, a six-year term, hand size, the midterm lean push from §10's priority, and governor appointments as a fifth route in.

    For the bill, change nothing. The threshold sweep moved passage 63/16/0 across 50/60/67 — but holding the threshold at 60% and varying only who is at the table moves it 14% to 100%, a wider range than the sweep produced. The 16% figure is a property of the agent pool, and §12's intent — that the filibuster makes cooperation structurally necessary — is working exactly as written. What the simulator cannot supply is the cooperation.

    confidence: high on the Senate · the bill needs a table, not a parameter

The rules that measured dead

Three coverage sweeps were run late and should have been run first: instrument every named entry in §9's modifier stack, then the seven enumerated effect types, then §7's ten year-sequence steps, and see which never appear. Each one turned up a rule that had never fired — and a fourth thing measured dead that no sweep could have caught.

MechanicBeforeAfter
§12's card counters — the bill's electoral consequencenever recorded15.0 & 14.2 / 1000 races
Printed card text on 346 candidatesdecorativefires in 4.12% of races
The veto0.00 a game0.30 a game

The omnibill had no electoral consequence whatsoever. §12 says a vote places a counter on the card and the accumulated counters are read off at resolution; reactionGood was computed on every passing bill and discarded, and Declaration.crossBenched was read by the modifier builder and never set by anything. A player could vote for anything, forever, and never answer for it. Implemented — and still unmeasurable, because across 120 games zero contested generals featured a candidate carrying a bill record. On a 92%-walkover board two rare things almost never coincide.

Every card's belief text was decoration. The brief asks what share of printed abilities ever change an outcome; the honest answer was all of it. Hooks are now written for the cases the design names by hand. The bug caught on the way in matters more than the feature: the conditional handler checked state, round and office and ignored its own identity condition, so an effect written as "a bonus in Catholic districts" fired in every race — 61 times per 1000 before the fix, 9.75 after. Effects that ignore their own conditions are worse than no effects.

The veto had never been exercised, because every agent's veto() returned false. The brief lists it as a cut candidate on the theory that it is only rational in split-government years, which may be rare. Implemented to §12's stated case it fires 0.30 times a game, and split government is 23% of games. The prior is right and the rule survives.

And one office measured dead without being unimplemented at all. Winners and losers held governorships at 1.02× — exactly neutral — so it was written up as an office nobody should plan around. That was measured against a field in which nobody sought the line the office exists for. §11 says governors "carry incumbency into Senate and presidential runs"; an agent built around that — take governorships, then run those same cards for Senate — changes the picture completely.

FieldGovernor advantageSenate advantage
Lookahead + Greedy + HouseFarm + Random1.07×3.55×
Launchpad + Greedy + SenateFlood + Heterodox2.39×1.59×

Same engine, same rules. The step-up converts a cheap office into an expensive one, and the Senate's advantage falls correspondingly. Launchpad wins 68–73% of four-player games against a 25% fair share, and 26.7% of a six-way round robin against stronger opposition — strong but not dominant, which is a healthy shape for a strategic line. Odd-year governorships are not the source: KY, LA, MS, NJ and VA had never had a race run at all, because openRaces() computed them and tick() only held elections in even years — 1,039 governor races in even years, 0 in odd — and turning them on makes Launchpad win less (68.3% against 72.8%). The step-up is the whole line.

A "dead" measurement is a claim about the agents, not about the design. The governorship joins incumbency, the presidency, the veto, the bill counters and the card text on the list of things that measured dead — with the difference that those five were unimplemented and this one was merely unplayed. A simulator can only find the strategies its agents already know.
And one sweep result was false. The same pass reported the Fed firing 0.00 times a game. It fires in about five: interactiveTick logged the Fed tightening and the headless tick did not, so the sweep was reading an instrument only one of two code paths wrote to. Trusted, it would have put a confident false claim that §13's entire Fed mechanism was dead into the section whose whole purpose is finding dead rules. A coverage sweep is only as good as the instrument it reads, and a log written by one code path and not its twin is not an instrument.

The findings are executable, and they expire

Prose does not know when it has stopped being true — establishing that took eight corrections. The load-bearing findings are now predicates under findings/, one module each: the design-doc question it answers, a predicate() that re-derives its claims from scratch with no cached numbers, a verdict() evaluated fresh on those claims, and a prose headline that is explicitly a stamped snapshot — true as of its stampedAt, on the engine in stampedOn, and no later. node sim/findings.ts re-runs every one and grades it.

GradeMeaning
HOLDSthe predicate still returns what the headline claims
STALEthe engine moved. The headline is now historical and the predicate is the current truth — information, not an error
BROKENthe predicate could not run at all

--restamp rewrites the stamped values and the date in place, and is the only sanctioned way for a headline to change. One rule beyond that: a finding that recommends a shipped config must declare it and check it, reading the file from disk as a zero-tolerance claim — findings/well-formed.test.ts fails any finding that names a dependency it does not read back. So decay-push-tradeoff.ts asserts that as-written-plus.json still ships the 2/3/4 push table and the annual decay it selects, and the config cannot outlive its evidence.

This is what caught the incumbency error. Converting the prose to a predicate forced it to name which population it measured, and naming it exposed that the prose never had. A sentence can hold two incompatible numbers for six findings; a function cannot.

What could not be tested

Everything social, and the brief says so itself. Negotiation before the bill, VP horse-trading, coalition-building for impeachment, table politics against a runaway leader. Three findings turn on it directly:

The bill's 16% pass rate is measured against agents that cannot negotiate, and §12's whole design intent is that they would. Impeachment's 0% rate is measured against agents that vote the party line, when removal is precisely a cross-party coalition. And the runaway has no mechanical brake, so the design is relying on players ganging up — which may well be enough, and cannot be shown here.

Do not lower the filibuster threshold, and do not add a catch-up mechanic, on this evidence. Play them with people first.
